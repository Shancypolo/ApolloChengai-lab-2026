## Worldview

The story takes place on a small Caribbean island nation in the early 1970s. The island is humid, mountainous, politically independent, and still divided between older rural life and a government determined to modernize through roads, electricity, concrete construction, and large public works.

A river valley in the island's interior is about to be deliberately flooded by a newly completed hydroelectric dam. The project is expected to bring electricity and irrigation to thousands of people, but several old communities have been evacuated to create the reservoir. The flooding begins the next day.

By the final chapter, floodwater submerges the entire island. The cause is not confirmed as supernatural; the story must continue through the submergence and show what happens to the protagonist and his photographs afterward.

Most of the valley is already empty.

Homes stand with their doors open. Pale rectangles remain where family photographs once hung. Furniture has been carried away, but small useless things remain behind: cups, calendars, broken toys, empty bottles, old newspapers, nails in walls.

The protagonist grew up here.

Almost nothing else about him is confirmed.

His history appears through familiarity. He knows shortcuts without searching for them. He remembers which stairs creak and where the river deepens. Some people recognize him immediately. Others stare at his camera as if they remember it too. He sometimes stops before ordinary places with no obvious importance and photographs nothing.

He never speaks.

People speak to him anyway. Some seem accustomed to his silence. Others hesitate as though expecting an answer. He communicates through gestures, expressions, and occasionally by writing something brief, but the story never explains why he does not speak.

His camera is old and heavily used. Its origin is unclear. The metal is worn smooth in certain places, and he handles it with the ease of something that has been near him for years.

He has enough film for only **five photographs**.

There is no replacement film available before the flood.

That makes every photograph a permanent decision.

At first, the obvious places seem worthy: the church, the cemetery, the bridge, the old sugar mill. But smaller things begin competing for his attention. A bedroom wall marked with children's heights. A mango tree beside an empty house. A barber chair facing a cracked mirror. A dog asleep beneath an abandoned bus stop.

He cannot preserve both the famous places and the private ones.

The camera becomes less a tool for documenting the valley than a way of deciding what the valley means.

The island's government presents the dam as necessary progress. Radio announcements celebrate new electricity, new factories, irrigation, and national independence. Officials speak of the valley mostly through numbers: acreage, megawatts, water capacity, relocation totals.

Those numbers are not entirely dishonest.

The dam may genuinely improve life elsewhere on the island.

The story's conflict comes from the fact that something useful can still destroy something irreplaceable.

Technology is limited and physical. Radios are common, telephones are not. News travels through newspapers, buses, letters, gossip, and government broadcasts. Photography is expensive because film and chemicals must be imported.

A photograph cannot be checked immediately.

He may waste one of his five exposures without knowing it.

The valley itself is green and enclosed by volcanic ridges. Cane grows along lower fields. Mango, breadfruit, palms, and ceiba trees surround houses. Roads are narrow and sun-bleached. Buildings combine old plaster walls, wooden shutters, tiled floors, corrugated roofs, and newer concrete additions.

The evacuation has disturbed ordinary life without completely erasing it.

A radio may still be playing in an empty café.

A clock may continue ticking inside an abandoned house.

Laundry may remain on a line because nobody considered it worth taking.

A half-finished game of dominoes may still sit on a table.

These details make the valley feel less abandoned than interrupted.

Sound matters strongly because the protagonist contributes no spoken voice. The world around him is filled with cicadas, church bells, trucks carrying possessions away, distant machinery from the dam, baseball commentary from radios, wind through cane, barking dogs, and waves against the coast.

As the day continues, human sounds become rarer.

The island has a strong oral culture of songs, family stories, legends, religious traditions, and local superstition. Catholic saints exist comfortably beside stories about river spirits, warnings carried through dreams, and old beliefs surrounding certain trees and stretches of water.

The story can contain a very slight magical element without confirming that anything supernatural is real.

After the photographs are developed, some may appear to contain details he does not remember seeing.

A person near an empty doorway.

Children beside a closed school.

A distant boat where the river should have been empty.

All of these could be explained by reflections, damaged film, double exposure, light leaks, or imperfect memory.

The story never decides for the reader.

The island's deeper theme is memory.

People have remembered the valley for generations without cameras. They remember houses through who lived inside them, trees through events that happened beneath them, roads through accidents, songs, arguments, weddings, storms, and deaths.

Photography appears more permanent.

But it is not.

Film can decay.

Paper can fade.

Names can be forgotten.

The protagonist's five photographs therefore cannot actually save the valley.

They can only preserve five fragments of it.

His central problem is not finding five beautiful places.

It is deciding which five losses deserve to become visible.

### Flood Aftermath

The story may end in any outcome. Final chapter must continue until floodwater has submerged the entire island, then clearly establish where the protagonist ends up and what becomes of his photographs. If he took none, state that no photographs exist and account for his camera and unused film. Photographs may be lost, damaged, recovered, or preserved; their fate must be clear rather than left as a cliffhanger.
